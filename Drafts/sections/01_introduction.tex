\section{Introduction}

Marriage between individuals from different groups, known as exogamy,
represents one of the key pathways to social and cultural integration.
Among its various forms, interethnic marriage is particularly salient in
today's multiethnic societies,
where individuals traverse traditional ethnic demarcations and commingle their
diverse social, economic, and cultural capital.
This boundary crossing carries significance far beyond individual
marriage--it both reflects and actively shapes broader ethnic relations.
As an outcome of these unions, the share of multiethnic households increases,
and their children often navigate multiple cultural identities,
contributing to growing demographic and cultural diversity across successive
generations.
Given these profound implications, interethnic marriage has long been recognized
as "the most sensitive index for measuring social distance across ethnic groups"
\cite{gordon_assimilation_1988} and "the inevitable accompaniment of [ethnic]
social organization's integration \cite{simpson_racial_1987}."

The Chinese context provides a particularly rich setting for examining
interethnic marriage dynamics.
Besides the Han majority, China is home to 55 officially recognized minority
ethnic groups, comprising, as of 2020, 8.89\% of the total population.
While this diverse ethnic landscape evolved over centuries, state policy played
a decisive role in its modern form when, in 1953, the newly establishd People's
Republic of China launched the Ethnic Identificaiton project, a massive
undertaking to identify and categorize various ethnic gorups based on language,
religion, culture, and territory.
Over 400 minority ethnic groups initially sought recognition, but were narrowed
down to 55, a process that was not finalized until 1982.
Cultural distance to the Han majority is diverse across the ethnic spectrum.
Some ethnic groups (e.g., Hui, Manchu, Mongolian) have been highly assimilated,
with intermarriage with Han spouses being a relatively common practice,
whereas other groups (e.g., Kazakh, Uyghur) maintain strong cultural, religious,
and terrotorial distinctions from Han Chinese.
For these latter groups in particularly, ethnoreligious identities represent a
cherished cultural heritage that they actively preserve, both as a source of
collective meaning and a marker of distinctiveness.
This leads many to view interethnic marriage as a potential threat to their
ethnic solidarity.

Marriage choices involve not only culturally loaded identities but also
instrumental considerations.
Social boundaries drawn by ethnicity can be bridged--especially in modern
societies--by achieved status markers like educational attainment.
Scholars have identified two key mechanisms through which education facilitates
intermarriage.
First, educational assortative mating creates opporunities for boundary
crossing, as individuals with similar educational levels often share compatible
values, beliefs, and knowledge structures.
These preferences make them more likely to intermarry across group lines,
especially among those with higher education and in relatively assimilated
groups that prioritize educational achievement over identities.
Second, status exchange theory suggests that members of differnet groups
strategically leverage their educational attainment to compensate for status
differentials, particularly among groups with salient identities but distinct
positions in the status hierarchy.
Both patterns are evident in the American context, where intermarriages with
Whites increased significantly across levels of educational homogamy among Asian
Americans, and those between African Americans and Whites exhibited patterns of
status exchange.

In China, status hierarchy across ethnicities is far more complicated than
simple socioeconomic disparities would suggest.
Some groups, such as the Koreans, are high achievers in educational
attainment--well above the Han majority, whereas others, particularly those
residing in rural and western regions, face persistent, and even widening,
educational and economic disadvantages.
To address these disparities, the state has implemented, since the 1980s,
favorable policies for ethnic minorities, spanning multiple domains sucha as
fertility rights, political representation, employment opporunities, and
educational access.
Some of these policy benefits extend beyond individual minorities to their
family members.
For example, under certain conditions, interethnic couples were exempted from
the historical One-Child Policy, and their children, when registered as ethnic
minorities, receive extra points in the national college entrance exam as well
as tuition fee waivers adn living expense stipends once enrolled.
Such policy advantages have made these minorities highly desired as marriage
partners, despite their lower socioeconomic status.
State intervention has created nuanced status dynamics in the marriage market,
incentivizing varying patterns of status exchange where the majority status is
not necessarily the most desired.

In this article, we provide the first nationwide study of interethnic
marriage, using micro-level census data from 1982 to 2010.
While previous research has examined marriage patterns in specific regions or
among certain ethnic groups, a comprehensive national analysis is essential for
systematic comparisons across time, space, and ethnic groups.
Our analysis unfolds in three stages.
First, we document the overall trends in interethnic marriage and their
variations across genders and ethnic groups.
Next, after controlling for ethnic compositions, we examine ethnic boundaries
as indicated by minority ethnic groups' preferences for or against intermarrying
with Han Chinese.
Finally, we explore the role of educational attainment in transcending
boundaries with the Han majority, focusing on patterns of educational
assortative mating and status exchange.
Through these analyses, we aim to provide a deeper understanding of how cultural
preservation, social mobility, and state-structured incentives collectively
shape the dynamics of interethnic marriage and, by extension, the broader
process of ethnic integration and stratification in contemporary China.
