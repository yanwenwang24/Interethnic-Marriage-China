\subsubsection{Variation in interethnic marriage across ethnic groups and periods}

Table~\ref{tab:goodness-fit1} summarizes the goodness-of-fit statistics for
log-linear models predicting marriages based on couples' ethnic pairings over
time.
These statistics include the log-likelihood ratio chi-square statistics ($G^2$),
degrees of freedom ($df$), index of dissimilarity (ID) between observed and
predicted numbers, and Bayesian information criterion (BIC).
Models with negative BIC values are preferred over the saturated model
(where $df$, $G^2$, and BIC equal 0).

The baseline model (Model 1), following Equation 1, assumes random marital
sorting by ethnic group.
This model shows poor fit, with an exceptionally large BIC value and over 10\%
of cases misclassified, confirming that mate selection between ethnic groups is
not random.

We then introduce ethnic parameters sequentially.
Model 1a incorporates a dummy variable distinguishing intra- from inter-ethnic
marriages, reducing the BIC score by 93.9\% (from 1,206,190 to 73,062) and
misclassified cases from 10.98\% to 1.74\%, suggesting statistically significant
associations between spouses' ethnic groups.
Model 1b distinguishes between Han-minority and minority-minority
intermarriages, yielding a 27.44\% drop in BIC score.
Models 1c and 1d differentiate intra-ethnic marriages by ethnic group and
Han-minority intermarriages by minority group, respectively.
Both show significant improvement over Model 1b, with BIC score reductions of
93.61\% and 94.24\%.
Model 1e combines parameters from Models 1c and 1d, further reducing the BIC
score to 914.

We then examine gender differences in Han-minority intermarriages
(Models 2a-2h).
None shows improvement over Model 1d, suggesting no significant gender
differences in minority individuals' preferences regarding Han intermarriage
after controlling for gender differences in ethnic composition.

In Models 3a-3e, we account for changes over time by interacting year with
ethnic parameters from Models 1a-1e.
All yield negative BIC scores, with Model 3d showing the lowest (-2,249).
This model, which differentiates Han-minority intermarriage by ethnic group and
interacts with years, provides the best fit and is selected for further
analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:inter_odds} illustrates changes in log-transformed expected
number of intermarriages with the Han majority from 1982 to 2010, using
intra-ethnic marriage as the reference level.
For clearer interpretation, we exponentialized the coefficients to compute
intermarriage ratios--the expected number of intermarriages with Han per 1,000
intra-ethnic marriages.
After controlling for ethnic compositions, all minority groups showed lower
propensity for Han intermarriage than would be expected under random sorting,
though the magnitude varied across groups.

In 1982, the Manchu showed the highest ratio at 88.57, while the Kazakh and
Uyghur showed the lowest at 0.03 and 0.04, respectively.
Other groups fell between these extremes: Southern minorities (26.21),
Mongolian (17.96), Hui (9.33), Tibetan (2.73), and Korean (1.00).

Over time, these ethnic boundaries became more permeable for most minority
groups except the Manchu.
The Korean population showed the most dramatic opening of ethnic borders, with
their intermarriage ratio rising from 1.00 to 8.62 by 2010.
Similar trends--more pronounced in absolute terms--were observed among the
Mongolian and Southern minorities, with ratios rising to 43.92 and 52.64,
respectively.
The Hui experienced moderate increases between 1982 and 1990, after which their
ratios stabilized.

The Tibetan population showed a nonlinear pattern, with intermarriage ratios
peaking at 6.45 in 2000 before declining to 3.98 in 2010.
The Kazakh and Uyghur maintained consistently low ratios throughout the period,
indicating persistent ethnic boundaries with the Han majority.

The Manchu were unique in showing a declining trend, contrary to the generally
increasing acceptance of Han intermarriage by other minority ethnic groups.
Their intermarriage ratio dropped from 88.57 in 1982 to 60.04 in 2010; however,
it remained amongst the highest.

These findings suggest persistent but shifting ethnic boundaries in China.
After accounting for structural constraints, all minority groups exhibited
preferences against Han intermarriage, but these preferences varied
substantially in strength--spanning from the most open (Manchu) to the most
closed (Uyghur and Kazakh) groups.
Most ethnic boundaries weakened over time, though at varying rates and with
notable exceptions.
The contrasting trajectories--from the dramatic opening of Korean-Han boundaries
to the exceptional closing of Manchu-Han boundaries--suggest that modernization
has affected different ethnic groups' integration with the Han majority in
distinct ways, reflecting the persistence, strengthening, and dissolution of
ethnic boundaries in contemporary China.
